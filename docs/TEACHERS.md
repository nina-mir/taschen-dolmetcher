# For Teachers

A practical guide to using *Taschendolmetscher · Revisited* in the classroom.

## What this is

*Taschendolmetscher · Revisited* is a language game built around a small artifact: the *Taschendolmetscher für Frontsoldaten — Russisch*, a German–Russian phrasebook issued to Wehrmacht soldiers on the Eastern Front in 1941. The booklet contained the operational vocabulary of occupation: phrases for demanding identity papers, food, information, surrender.

The project asks students to learn those phrases — German, Russian, English — and pairs each one with the historical record of what the phrases were really for: photographs of the Eastern Front, partisan testimony, art made by Holocaust survivors, and reportage by Soviet war correspondents.

It is not a Holocaust education site. It is not a language-textbook chapter. It sits between the two, and that placement is the point.

## What it asks of students

Students who use this project are doing three things at once:

1. **Acquiring vocabulary** in two unfamiliar languages, through standard flashcard mechanics (multiple choice and free response).
2. **Reading archival material** — captions, dates, attributions — as primary-source historians do.
3. **Confronting the gap** between the bureaucratic neutrality of a phrasebook ("Where are the partisans?") and the historical reality of what such a phrase produced.

The third item is the educational core. The first two are the scaffolding.

## Content advisory

This project contains photographs, drawings, and paintings depicting the violence of the Eastern Front and the Holocaust. Specifically:

- Photographs of mass execution sites (Kerch, Liepaja, Minsk, Rostov)
- Documentation of mass graves, including Bergen-Belsen
- Images of public executions and hangings, including the partisan Zoya Kosmodemyanskaya
- Artwork by Holocaust survivors depicting the camps and ghettos, including one image (Haskiel Kujawski, *Like Animals*) that depicts sexual violence against women
- Photographs of victims' bodies, sometimes in advanced states of decomposition

Approximately one-third of the images carry these characteristics. Each is flagged in the underlying data with a `sensitiveContent` marker, and the interface blurs flagged images until the student chooses to reveal them. A "show all" toggle is available, but is not the default.

**You should preview the material before assigning it.** No content note can substitute for your own judgment about your specific students and your specific institutional context.

## Suggested uses, by audience

### High school (grades 10–12)

The project works well as a supplement to units on the Second World War, the Holocaust, or 20th-century European history. It is probably not appropriate before grade 10 without significant preparation.

**One class period (45–55 minutes):**

- Open with the artifact itself: show students the original phrasebook page (linked in the project's "About" section) before they touch the game. Five minutes of "what do you notice about this object?" — its size, its imperatives, the absence of pleasantries.
- Give students 15 minutes with the multiple-choice mode. Tell them to keep the sensitive-content blur on by default and to reveal only images they choose to engage with.
- Reconvene. Ask: *which phrases did the booklet contain that surprised you? Which ones didn't?*
- Close on one image, chosen in advance — I'd suggest the Baltermants *Grief* photograph (Kerch, 1942), which is grave but not graphic, and rewards slow looking. Ten minutes of discussion on a single image is more valuable than thirty images skimmed.

**A homework assignment:** ask students to write a one-paragraph caption for one image they encountered, in the voice of a museum curator. This forces them to consider what *should* be said about each artifact, separate from what they personally feel.

**Skip these images if you have students with recent personal exposure to violence, displacement, or genocide:** the Kujawski *Like Animals* painting; the Vlasov-army collaborators-with-corpses photograph; the public-hanging images. The project remains coherent without them.

### University

The project supports several disciplines and several depths of engagement.

**For language instructors (German, Russian, Slavic studies):** the project is a primary-source companion to standard intermediate-level vocabulary. The imperative-mood German is a useful counterweight to the polite-register vocabulary of most textbooks. A 50-minute session can productively focus on the *grammatical voice* of occupation — who is the subject, what is the verb form, who is being addressed — and what those choices reveal about the speaker's position.

**For history instructors:** the project pairs naturally with units on the Holocaust by bullets (the Shoah in the East), the Soviet experience of the Great Patriotic War, or the historiography of memory. Recommended companion readings include Timothy Snyder's *Bloodlands*, Vasily Grossman's wartime journalism (*A Writer at War*, edited by Beevor and Vinogradova), and the relevant chapters of Yitzhak Arad's *The Holocaust in the Soviet Union*.

**For museum studies, digital humanities, or curatorial practice:** the project itself is the text. What does it mean to encode the difference between a phrasebook's voice and a witness's voice in CSS? Why does the interface blur some images and not others? Who decided? On what evidence? These are productive questions for a graduate seminar.

**For ethics, philosophy, or religious studies:** the project can frame a discussion of *what we owe the dead* in representational media. Compare with the debates over the U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum's design decisions in the 1980s, or with Claude Lanzmann's *Shoah* and its refusal of archival imagery.

## Things to anticipate

**Students will ask whether the phrases are accurate.** They are — drawn from the actual 1941 booklet. Where translations have been smoothed for the game, that is documented in the underlying data. If a student wants to verify, the original is digitized at the Museum Digital Berlin record linked in the project's About page.

**Students may ask why the project doesn't include phrases from a Russian counter-phrasebook.** The Red Army did issue Russian–German phrasebooks for its own soldiers, with their own characteristic imperatives. The asymmetry of the current project — only the occupier's phrasebook — is a deliberate curatorial choice, but it is a choice, and a thoughtful student is right to notice it.

**Some students will be uncomfortable.** Some will be uncomfortable in ways that surprise them. Build a way for them to step out of the activity without performing engagement — a parallel reading, a writing prompt that doesn't require looking, an option to work on the language layer alone. The blur-by-default UI helps; your assignment design should help further.

**Some students will not be uncomfortable enough.** This is more difficult to address and may be the more common case. The project's quietness is its argument — it does not perform horror at the viewer, and it trusts the viewer to do the work. For students who have not been trained to do that work, the artifacts can read as flat. A discussion question that helps: *What would this image have looked like if it had been made by the perpetrator instead of the witness? What changes?*

## What this project is not

It is not a complete Holocaust curriculum. It does not address the camps in any depth, the perpetrators in any depth, or the postwar reckoning at all. It is one small object — a phrasebook — surrounded by what that object touched.

Pair it with a fuller curriculum if you are teaching the period. Use it on its own if you are teaching a specific question about language, occupation, or witness.

## Brief bibliography for further reading

- Timothy Snyder, *Bloodlands: Europe Between Hitler and Stalin* (2010)
- Vasily Grossman, *A Writer at War*, ed. Beevor and Vinogradova (2005)
- Vasily Grossman and Ilya Ehrenburg, eds., *The Black Book: The Ruthless Murder of Jews by German-Fascist Invaders Throughout the Temporarily-Occupied Regions of the Soviet Union and in the Death Camps of Poland During the War of 1941–1945*
- Yitzhak Arad, *The Holocaust in the Soviet Union* (2009)
- Anatoly Kuznetsov, *Babi Yar: A Document in the Form of a Novel* (1966, restored 1970)
- Wendy Lower, *The Ravine: A Family, a Photograph, a Holocaust Massacre Revealed* (2021)
- David Shneer, *Through Soviet Jewish Eyes: Photography, War, and the Holocaust* (2010) — especially good on Baltermants, Khaldey, and the Soviet-Jewish photographers represented in this project

For artwork and survivor testimony: the Ghetto Fighters' House Museum (Israel) and Yad Vashem maintain extensive digital archives, both linked throughout the project's source material.
